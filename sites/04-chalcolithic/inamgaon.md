- **Location**: Inamgaon, on the Ghod river, a tributary of the Bhima, in Pune district, Maharashtra; excavated over 1968-83 by H. D. Sankalia, M. K. Dhavalikar and Z. D. Ansari.
- **Period/Level**: A Chalcolithic sequence covering the Malwa and Jorwe phases, with the later Jorwe phase dated about 1300-1000 BCE.
- **Key Finds & Significance**: The most completely excavated Chalcolithic village in India, and the site on which almost all reconstructions of Chalcolithic social organisation, craft specialisation and religion are based.
  - Inamgaon was a large settlement of over a hundred houses with numerous graves, fortified and surrounded by a moat. In the earlier phase there were large mud houses with ovens and circular pit houses; in the later phase a house of five rooms, four rectangular and one circular, stood at the centre of the settlement and may have belonged to a chief, with a granary close by that may have stored tribute in kind.
  - Craft specialisation is unusually visible: besides farmers there were potters, smiths, ivory carvers, lime makers and terracotta artisans, and the craftsmen lived on the western fringe while the chief probably lived at the centre, a spatial pattern read as social distance.
  - The early Chalcolithic farmers obtained their food by farming, hunting and fishing; X-ray diffraction analysis of pottery indicates that Inamgaon supplied earthen pots to Walki, 27 km away, direct evidence of inter-village exchange.
  - A headless female figurine, comparable to mother goddess figures of western Asia, was recovered; the dead were buried in urns beneath house floors in a north-south position, an adult being buried with pottery and some copper, and there were no separate cemeteries as among the Harappans.
  - Plant and animal remains indicate the onset of an extremely arid phase around 1000 BCE which forced the farmers to abandon their homes and take to pastoral nomadism, the clearest Indian case of climate-driven settlement collapse.
