- **Location**: Gauda, later Lakhnauti and Gaur, Malda district, West Bengal, on an abandoned channel of the Ganga astride the present India-Bangladesh border - the deltaic heartland of Bengal.
- **Period/Level**: Capital of Shashanka's Gauda kingdom in the early 7th century CE; the seat from which the Palas ruled after Gopala's election c. 750; then the Senas'; and from 1204, when Bakhtiyar Khalji took it, the capital of Muslim Bengal as Lakhnauti, held by the Ilyas Shahi and Husain Shahi sultans until Sher Shah sacked it in 1538 and plague emptied it in 1575.
- **Key Finds & Significance**: One site carrying a thousand years of Bengal's history - the only recorded election of a king in early India, and the capital of an independent Bengal Sultanate.
  - The Khalimpur copper plate states that the people, worn down by *matsyanyaya* - the law of the fishes, where the strong devour the weak - chose Gopala to be king. Taranatha's later Tibetan account says the same. An elected founder is unique in Indian dynastic records and is the single most cited fact about the Palas.
  - The Palas ruled Bengal and Bihar for some four centuries and were the last great royal patrons of Buddhism in India, endowing Nalanda, Vikramashila and Odantapuri and sending Atisha Dipankara to Tibet. Pala bronze and stone sculpture defines the eastern Indian style.
  - Shashanka, who preceded them, is remembered in Buddhist sources as a persecutor and in political history as the king who killed Harsha's brother Rajyavardhana - the link between Gauda and Thanesar.
  - Under the sultans, Bengal was effectively independent of Delhi from 1342; distance, the monsoon and the rivers repeatedly defeated attempts at control, making it the standard example of Sultanate provincial breakaway. Its architecture is brick, not stone, because the delta has no quarries: the curved *do-chala* cornice copied from the thatched hut, terracotta ornament and squat domes seen in the Dakhil Darwaza, the Firoz Minar and the Bara Sona Masjid of 1526. The same curved roof appears on Hindu temples at Bishnupur, so the form is regional rather than religious.
  - Alauddin Husain Shah, r. 1494-1519, patronised Bengali literature and employed Hindu officers; Rupa and Sanatana, later two of Chaitanya's Six Goswamis, served in his administration.
  - *Sources*: Upinder Singh, ch. 10; Thapar, *Early India*; Satish Chandra, *Medieval India* I.
