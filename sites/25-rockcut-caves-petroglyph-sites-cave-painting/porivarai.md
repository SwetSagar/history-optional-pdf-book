- **Location**: Porivarai, in the Karikkiyur complex of the Nilgiri hills near Kotagiri, Tamil Nadu - a rock shelter in the Western Ghats at about 1,500 m. The name means "the rock of writing" or "carved rock" in Tamil.
- **Period/Level**: Rock paintings assigned broadly to the late Neolithic and megalithic periods, roughly the 2nd millennium BCE to the early centuries CE, with some figures argued to be later. The Nilgiri megalithic cairns and cromlechs in the surrounding hills belong to the same broad horizon.
- **Key Finds & Significance**: The principal rock art site of the Nilgiris - the southern extremity of the Indian painted-shelter tradition.
  - The paintings, in white kaolin and red ochre, show human figures with headdresses and weapons, riders, cattle, deer and a range of symbols. Figures carrying what appear to be bows and standing beside horses suggest contact with the megalith-building communities of the peninsular uplands.
  - The Nilgiri material extends the map of Indian rock art south beyond the Deccan, and its imagery differs from both the Vindhyan hunting scenes and the cattle-centred art of the Bellary region - evidence that the tradition had distinct regional grammars rather than one subject repertoire.
  - The Nilgiris also hold a dense concentration of megalithic monuments excavated from the 19th century by Breeks and others, and the relationship between those burials and the shelter paintings is the site's open question.
  - The hills remain the home of the Toda, Kota, Kurumba and Irula communities, and Kurumba wall painting has been compared with the prehistoric work, though continuity is asserted more often than demonstrated.
  - *Sources*: Upinder Singh, ch. 2 and 6; ASI; *Indian Archaeology - A Review*.
