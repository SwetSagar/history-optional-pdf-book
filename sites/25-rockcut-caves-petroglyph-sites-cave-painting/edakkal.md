- **Location**: Edakkal, on Ambukuthi hill near Ambalavayal, Wayanad district, Kerala, at about 1,200 m in the Western Ghats - a cleft formed where a boulder fell across a fissure, so the "cave" is a natural rock shelter rather than an excavation.
- **Period/Level**: Petroglyphs of at least two phases, the earlier assigned to the Neolithic, roughly the 4th-3rd millennia BCE, and later carvings to the early historic period. Brahmi and Tamil-Brahmi inscriptions on the same walls date to about the 3rd-5th centuries CE. Reported by Fred Fawcett in 1890.
- **Key Finds & Significance**: The only substantial prehistoric petroglyph site in south India, and one of very few anywhere carved rather than painted.
  - The engravings are cut into the rock, not painted, which sets Edakkal apart from Bhimbetka, Lakhudiyar and the Vindhyan shelters. They show human figures with elaborate headdresses, wheels, carts, animals, tridents and geometric symbols.
  - Some signs have been compared with Indus symbols - a claim made repeatedly and never demonstrated. The honest position is that the resemblance is unproven and probably coincidental, and it should be reported as a claim rather than a finding.
  - The later Brahmi inscriptions, including one reading *Sri Vazhumi*, show the shelter still in use in the early historic period, so the site records continuous human attention across three thousand years.
  - Wayanad's position on the routes between the Malabar coast and the Deccan plateau explains why a hill shelter here attracted such sustained use; megalithic remains occur nearby.
  - *Sources*: Upinder Singh, ch. 3; R. S. Sharma, *India's Ancient Past*; ASI.
