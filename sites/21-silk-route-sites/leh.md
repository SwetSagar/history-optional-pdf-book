- **Location**: Leh, Ladakh, on the right bank of the Indus at about 3,500 m in the rain-shadow between the Zanskar and Ladakh ranges - the meeting point of the routes over the Karakoram to Yarkand, west to Kashmir, and east along the Indus to Tibet.
- **Period/Level**: A settlement of the Ladakh region from early times, and capital of the Namgyal dynasty from the 16th century. Leh Palace, a nine-storeyed structure modelled on the Potala, was built by Sengge Namgyal in the 17th century. The Treaty of Tingmosgang in 1684 fixed the Ladakh-Tibet frontier and the terms of its trade.
- **Key Finds & Significance**: The hinge of the trans-Himalayan trade, and the route by which Indian Buddhism reached Tibet and survived there.
  - Leh was the entrepot of the pashmina trade. The fine underwool of the Changthangi goat, raised on the western Tibetan plateau, was brought here and channelled by treaty to Kashmir, where it was woven into shawls. An entire luxury industry in Srinagar depended on a mountain route through Leh, and the Tingmosgang terms made that monopoly explicit.
  - The Karakoram route to Yarkand and Kashgar linked Ladakh to the Tarim basin oases, carrying silk, tea, gold, borax and charas in exchange - the southernmost limb of the Silk Route system.
  - Ladakhi Buddhism, in its Tibetan form, preserves the monastic and textual tradition transmitted from Kashmir and from the Indian mahaviharas. Monasteries at Hemis, Alchi, Thiksey and Lamayuru hold manuscripts, and Alchi's 11th-12th century murals preserve a Kashmiri painting style otherwise entirely lost in Kashmir itself.
  - *Sources*: Upinder Singh, ch. 10; Satish Chandra, *Medieval India* II; Janet Rizvi, *Trans-Himalayan Caravans*.
