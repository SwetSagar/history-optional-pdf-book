- **Location**: Kabul, eastern Afghanistan, in a high valley on the Kabul river between the Hindu Kush passes to the north and the Khyber to the east - the gate through which almost every land invasion of India has passed.
- **Period/Level**: A Hindu Shahi centre before the Ghaznavids; taken by Babur in 1504, who ruled from it for twenty-two years before Panipat in 1526 and was buried there at his own wish. Kabul and Kandahar were Mughal provinces, Kandahar contested repeatedly with the Safavids until lost in 1649.
- **Key Finds & Significance**: The Mughal dynasty's own homeland and the empire's strategic hinge - the reason so much Mughal effort went north-west rather than south.
  - Babur's *Baburnama* describes Kabul's markets in detail: caravans arriving from Ferghana, Turkestan, Khurasan and Hindustan, and the goods and fruits of each. It is one of the best first-person accounts of Central Asian trade, written by a ruler who knew the routes personally.
  - Retaining Kabul mattered because it commanded the passes. The Mughal obsession with Kandahar and the disastrous Balkh-Badakhshan campaign of 1646-47 under Shah Jahan, intended to recover Timur's Samarkand, both follow from this orientation - an enormous drain of treasure for territory the empire could not hold.
  - Babur asked to be buried at Kabul rather than in India, and the *Bagh-e-Babur* he laid out is the earliest Mughal garden, the prototype of the *charbagh* that reaches its Indian form at Agra and Lahore.
  - The city's position also made it the route by which Central Asian horses, essential to Indian cavalry, entered the subcontinent.
  - *Sources*: Satish Chandra, *Medieval India* II; J. L. Mehta, *Advanced Study in the History of Medieval India*; Babur, *Baburnama*.
