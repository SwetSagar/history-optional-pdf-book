- **Location**: Gingee or Senji, Villupuram district, Tamil Nadu, where three granite hills - Rajagiri, Krishnagiri and Chandrayandurg - are linked by a wall enclosing about 11 square km of ground between them.
- **Period/Level**: Begun under the Konar chiefs and the Cholas, and fortified on its present scale by the Gingee Nayaks under Vijayanagara in the 16th century. It passed to Bijapur in 1649, to Shivaji in 1677, to the Mughals in 1698, then to the Carnatic Nawabs, the French in 1750 and the British in 1761.
- **Key Finds & Significance**: The strongest fortress in the Deccan, and the base from which the Maratha state survived Aurangzeb's conquest of Maharashtra.
  - After Sambhaji's execution in 1689, Rajaram escaped to Gingee and ruled from here for eight years while Zulfiqar Khan besieged it. The Mughals took it only in 1698, by which time Maratha power had regenerated in the west. Gingee is therefore the answer to how the Maratha state outlasted the loss of its own homeland.
  - Its design uses three separate hill citadels with independent water and granaries, so that the fall of one did not end resistance - the reason the fort acquired the reputation Shivaji is said to have given it as the most impregnable in India.
  - The Kalyana Mahal, a square tower in the Nayak style, the granaries, and the cut-rock cisterns show what a self-sufficient garrison needed.
  - Raja Tej Singh, Desingh of the ballads, died in 1714 resisting the Nawab of Arcot, and the episode entered Tamil folk literature - an example of local memory preserving a minor chief's revolt.
  - *Sources*: Satish Chandra, *Medieval India* II; J. L. Mehta, *Advanced Study in the History of Medieval India*; ASI.
