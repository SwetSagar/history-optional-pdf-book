- **Location**: Takht-i-Bahi, Mardan district, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Pakistan, on a hill about 150 m above the Peshawar plain - in the heart of Gandhara, on the route between Pushkalavati and the Swat valley.
- **Period/Level**: Founded in the 1st century CE; an inscription dated in the reign of the Indo-Parthian king Gondophares points to c. 46 CE. Four building phases run to the 7th century, through the Kushana and Kidarite periods. Excavated from 1871 and by the ASI under Spooner and Hargreaves; UNESCO World Heritage, 1980.
- **Key Finds & Significance**: The most complete surviving Gandharan monastery - the one where the whole plan of a Buddhist establishment can still be read.
  - It has four distinct components: the Court of Stupas, a courtyard ringed by votive stupas; the monastic quadrangle of individual cells around an open court; an assembly hall and refectory with a kitchen and water tank; and a temple complex of many stupas on a higher terrace.
  - A later addition is a Tantric complex of dark cells with low openings, taken as evidence of Vajrayana practice in Gandhara - a rare architectural trace of esoteric Buddhism in the north-west.
  - Its schist and stucco sculpture is classic Gandhara: the Buddha in Graeco-Roman drapery with wavy hair and a halo, narrative friezes of the life, and Corinthian pilasters - the fusion of Hellenistic form with Buddhist content under Kushana patronage.
  - Its hilltop position, away from a city, kept it out of reach of later building and is why it survives when the plain sites do not.
  - *Sources*: Upinder Singh, ch. 8; John Marshall, *The Buddhist Art of Gandhara*; UNESCO.
