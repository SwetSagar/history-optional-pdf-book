- **Location**: Sigiriya, Matale district, central Sri Lanka - a 180 m granite monolith rising from the dry-zone plain, with a planned city at its foot.
- **Period/Level**: Caves around the rock carry drip-ledges and Brahmi inscriptions of c. 3rd century BCE-1st century CE, when they housed forest monks. King Kashyapa I made the rock his fortified capital in 477-495 CE; after his death it reverted to a monastery and was occupied until the 13th-14th centuries. UNESCO World Heritage, 1982.
- **Key Finds & Significance**: The best-preserved example of early Asian urban planning, and the closest surviving relative of the Ajanta painting tradition.
  - The plan is symmetrical and axial: water gardens with working fountains at the west, boulder and terraced gardens rising to the rock, and the palace on the summit - hydraulic engineering and landscape design integrated on a scale not matched in South Asia for centuries.
  - A colossal lion of brick and plaster once framed the stairway; only the paws survive, and give the rock its name, Sinhagiri.
  - Of a reputed five hundred figures in the western rock pocket, twenty-one apsaras or cloud maidens survive - painted in sweeping strokes with heavier pressure on one side and deepening tone towards the edge to model the form, a technique closely comparable to Ajanta, and evidence of a shared Indian Ocean painting tradition.
  - The polished Mirror Wall carries some 1,800 graffiti of the 8th-10th centuries, verses left by visitors - the earliest substantial body of Sinhala poetry and a rare record of ordinary literacy.
  - *Sources*: Senake Bandaranayake, *Sigiriya*; UNESCO World Heritage documentation; the *Culavamsa*.
