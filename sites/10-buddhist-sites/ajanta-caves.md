- **Location**: Ajanta, Aurangabad (Chhatrapati Sambhajinagar) district, Maharashtra, cut into a horseshoe-shaped basalt scarp above the Waghora, a tributary of the Tapi system, on the route from Pratishthana to the northern trade towns.
- **Period/Level**: Thirty caves in two phases separated by some five centuries. Phase I is Hinayana under the Satavahanas, c. 2nd-1st centuries BCE - Caves 9 and 10 (chaityas) and 8, 12, 13 and 15A (viharas). Phase II is Mahayana under the Vakatakas, c. 460-480 CE, in the reign of Harishena, and the site was abandoned soon after his fall. Rediscovered in 1819 by John Smith, an officer of the Madras Army.
- **Key Finds & Significance**: The only place where Indian painting of the classical age survives in quantity, and where the shift from aniconic to iconic Buddhism can be read on the walls.
  - The paintings are not true fresco but tempera on a dry ground of mud, cow dung and rice husk finished with lime - drawn in outline, filled in, then burnished. Five mineral colours dominate: red and yellow ochre, lime white, lamp black, terre verte, and lapis lazuli blue imported from Central Asia.
  - Subjects are Jataka and Avadana tales, not doctrine: Cave 1 has the Padmapani and Vajrapani bodhisattvas and the Mahajanaka Jataka; Cave 17 the Vessantara and Simhala Jatakas; Cave 16 the "Dying Princess", and an inscription of Varahadeva, Harishena's minister, recording its excavation.
  - Caves 19 and 26 are the Mahayana chaityas: apsidal halls with carved pillars, a stupa carrying a standing Buddha on its face, and elaborately carved facades. Cave 26 adds a seven-metre reclining Mahaparinirvana Buddha and the Temptation of Mara.
  - The narrative idiom descends from Sanchi and Bharhut; the Ajanta manner travels on to Bagh, Badami, Sittanavasal and Sigiriya. UNESCO World Heritage, 1983.
  - *Sources*: Upinder Singh, ch. 9; Walter Spink, *Ajanta: History and Development*; ASI.
