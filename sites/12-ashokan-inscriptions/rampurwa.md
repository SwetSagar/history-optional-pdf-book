- **Location**: Rampurva, West Champaran district, Bihar, near the Nepal border in the swampy plain north of the Gandak - on the royal road running from Pataliputra towards the Himalayan foothills.
- **Period/Level**: Two polished Chunar sandstone pillars, c. 243-242 BCE. One carries Pillar Edicts I-VI in Brahmi and Prakrit and is crowned by a lion; the second is uninscribed and carries a bull capital. Excavated by A. C. L. Carlleyle in 1877 and Daya Ram Sahni in 1907-08.
- **Key Finds & Significance**: The finest surviving Mauryan animal capital, and the clearest evidence of Achaemenid and Hellenistic motifs in Mauryan art.
  - The Rampurva bull, carved in the round in sandstone with the characteristic Mauryan polish, stands on an abacus decorated with a honeysuckle and palmette frieze - motifs of Greek and West Asian origin, not Indian - above a bell-shaped inverted lotus derived from Achaemenid Persepolis. It is now at Rashtrapati Bhavan, New Delhi.
  - That borrowed ornament is the heart of the long argument over Mauryan court art: whether the polish, the free-standing monolithic column and the animal capital were learnt from Persia after the fall of Achaemenid Iran, or developed from an Indian wooden tradition. The honeysuckle frieze is the strongest single piece of evidence for the foreign side.
  - The second pillar's lion is squatter and cruder than the Sarnath lions, which is used to argue a development within Mauryan sculpture rather than a single imported style.
  - The Champaran pillars - Rampurva, Lauriya Nandangarh, Lauriya Araraj - stand in a line and are usually read as marking a processional or pilgrimage route towards Lumbini and the Buddha's country.
  - *Sources*: Upinder Singh, ch. 7; Niharranjan Ray, *Maurya and Post-Maurya Art*; ASI.
