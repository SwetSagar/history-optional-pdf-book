- **Location**: Old Kandahar (Shar-i-Kuna), southern Afghanistan, in the Arghandab valley on the road from the Iranian plateau to the Indus - probably Alexandria in Arachosia, and the western limit of the empire.
- **Period/Level**: Three groups of texts. The Greek-Aramaic bilingual rock inscription, found in 1958, is dated "ten years after the consecration", c. 258 BCE. A Greek inscription found in 1963 carries a translation of parts of Major Rock Edicts 12 and 13. Further Aramaic inscriptions come from the same region.
- **Key Finds & Significance**: The only Ashokan texts in Greek - proof that the empire administered in the languages of its subjects, not only its own.
  - The bilingual calls the king "Piodasses" and announces that from the tenth year he made men pious, that all living things prosper, that he abstains from killing, and that hunters and fishermen have ceased - the Greek rendering translating dhamma as eusebeia, piety, a Greek religious concept.
  - That translation choice is the evidence usually cited for dhamma being a broad ethical idea capable of being carried across cultures, rather than a sectarian Buddhist doctrine.
  - Aramaic was the Achaemenid chancery language and Greek the language of the Seleucid successor population; their use here shows a Mauryan administration inheriting and working through Persian and Hellenistic bureaucratic practice in the north-west.
  - Major Rock Edict XIII names five Hellenistic kings - Antiochus II, Ptolemy II, Antigonus, Magas and Alexander - as recipients of Ashoka's dhamma missions, and this is the region through which those embassies would have passed.
  - *Sources*: Upinder Singh, ch. 7; D. C. Sircar, *Select Inscriptions*; Thapar, *Asoka and the Decline of the Mauryas*.
