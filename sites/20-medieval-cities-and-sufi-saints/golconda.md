- **Location**: Golconda, on a granite hill on the western edge of Hyderabad, Telangana - a citadel on the Deccan plateau above the Musi basin, with the Qutb Shahi tombs on the ridge outside.
- **Period/Level**: A Kakatiya mud fort rebuilt in stone by the Qutb Shahis from 1518, when Quli Qutb Shah declared independence from the Bahmanis. It was the capital until Hyderabad was founded in 1591, and fell to Aurangzeb in 1687 after a siege of eight months ended by bribery rather than assault.
- **Key Finds & Significance**: The diamond capital of the world, and the conquest that completed Aurangzeb's overextension in the Deccan.
  - Every great Indian diamond of the period - the Koh-i-Noor, the Hope, the Regent, the Orlov - came through Golconda's market from the Kollur and other mines along the Krishna. "Golconda" entered European languages as a byword for wealth. Tavernier's account of the mines and the trade is the standard source.
  - The fort's acoustics are famous: a clap at the Fateh Darwaza carries to the Bala Hisar pavilion at the summit, part of a designed system of defence and signalling.
  - Muhammad Quli Qutb Shah, who founded Hyderabad and built the Charminar in 1591, wrote poetry in Dakhni Urdu, and the Qutb Shahi court is central to the emergence of Urdu as a literary language in the Deccan rather than in the north.
  - Aurangzeb's annexation of Golconda in 1687, after Bijapur in 1686, removed the buffer states that had contained the Marathas and committed the empire to a war it could not end - the standard argument that the Deccan campaigns caused the Mughal decline.
  - *Sources*: Satish Chandra, *Medieval India* II; J. L. Mehta, *Advanced Study in the History of Medieval India*; Tavernier, *Travels in India*.
