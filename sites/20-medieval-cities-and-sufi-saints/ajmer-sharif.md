- **Location**: Ajmer, Rajasthan, in a valley of the Aravallis below Taragarh hill beside the Ana Sagar lake - the Chahamana capital after Sambhar, and a natural pass town between the desert and eastern Rajasthan.
- **Period/Level**: The dargah of Khwaja Moinuddin Chishti, who came to India in the late 12th century and died at Ajmer in 1236. The shrine buildings are largely of the Khalji, Tughlaq and Mughal periods, with Akbar and Shah Jahan adding mosques and gateways; the annual *urs* marks the saint's death anniversary.
- **Key Finds & Significance**: The principal Sufi shrine in India, and the institutional centre of the Chishti order.
  - The Chishtis are defined by what they refused: state office, land grants and royal stipends. They lived on *futuh*, unsolicited offerings, kept open *langar*, and used *sama*, devotional music, as a spiritual practice - the last controversial with the orthodox ulama and the reason Chishti khanqahs drew musicians and poets.
  - That refusal is best understood against the Suhrawardis at Multan and Uch, who accepted office, held property and worked with the state. The Chishti-Suhrawardi contrast is the standard frame for any question on Sufism in the Delhi Sultanate.
  - Akbar walked to Ajmer from Agra more than once after Salim's birth, endowed the shrine and installed a great cooking cauldron. Imperial patronage of a saint who had rejected patronage is a neat illustration of how Mughal legitimacy borrowed from popular devotion.
  - The shrine's following has always crossed religious lines, which is why it features in discussions of composite culture - though the Chishti chain from Moinuddin through Qutbuddin Bakhtiyar Kaki, Baba Farid and Nizamuddin Auliya is what the exam usually wants.
  - *Sources*: Satish Chandra, *Medieval India* I; J. L. Mehta, *Advanced Study in the History of Medieval India* I.
