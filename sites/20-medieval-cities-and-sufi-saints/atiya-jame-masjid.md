- **Location**: The Atia Jame Masjid at Atia, Delduar upazila, Tangail district, Bangladesh, on the bank of the old Jhenai channel in the Brahmaputra floodplain - deep in the deltaic interior, away from any stone source.
- **Period/Level**: Built in 1609 CE by Sa'id Khan Panni, a local chieftain, in memory of the Sufi Shah Baba Kashmiri. Brick throughout, with moulded terracotta ornament; repaired in the 19th century and again later, which accounts for its present condition.
- **Key Finds & Significance**: A compact demonstration that Islamic architecture in Bengal is a regional idiom rather than an imported one.
  - The building is a single-aisled, three-domed mosque of the standard Bengal type, but its defining feature is the curved *do-chala* cornice - the roofline of a thatched bamboo hut reproduced in brick. The same curve appears on the Hindu temples at Bishnupur built in the same century, so the form belongs to Bengal, not to a religion.
  - Brick and terracotta were used because the delta has no building stone. The walls carry moulded panels of rosettes, chains, lamps and foliage in the same technique the temple builders used for narrative scenes - one craft tradition serving two faiths.
  - It stands between the Sultanate mosques of Gaur and the later Mughal-influenced work in Dhaka, and is usually cited to show the regional style continuing after Mughal annexation in 1576 rather than being displaced by it.
  - Patronage by a local chieftain, not a sultan or emperor, makes it evidence of how far down the social scale architectural ambition reached.
  - *Sources*: Satish Chandra, *Medieval India* II; Department of Archaeology, Bangladesh.
