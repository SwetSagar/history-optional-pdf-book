- **Location**: Multan, southern Punjab, Pakistan, in the doab near the old course of the Ravi close to its junction with the Chenab - the hinge between Sindh, the Punjab and the routes to Khurasan.
- **Period/Level**: Ancient Mulasthana, famous for its sun temple, described by Xuanzang in the 7th century and by Al-Biruni before Mahmud of Ghazni destroyed it. Under Arab and then Qarmatian rule, it became a frontier province of the Delhi Sultanate; Nasiruddin Qubacha held it in the early 13th century. The shrine of Bahauddin Zakariya, d. 1262, is its Sufi centre.
- **Key Finds & Significance**: The seat of the Suhrawardi order in India - the counter-model to Chishti practice - and the Sultanate's principal western trade city.
  - Bahauddin Zakariya established the Suhrawardi silsila here on the opposite principle to the Chishtis: he accepted royal patronage and office, held substantial property, maintained contact with the Sultanate, and did not practise poverty. Setting Multan against Ajmer is the cleanest way to show that Indian Sufism was not one thing.
  - The tomb of Shah Rukn-e-Alam, his grandson, built in the 1320s, is among the great monuments of Tughlaq architecture - an octagonal brick mausoleum with sloping buttresses and glazed blue and white tile, and the model for later Sindhi and Punjabi tomb building.
  - As a Sultanate province Multan controlled the caravan traffic to Khurasan and Central Asia, and was the point at which Mongol pressure on the north-west was absorbed for over a century.
  - The destruction of the sun temple by Mahmud, and the earlier Arab practice of leaving it standing to tax pilgrims, are often paired to show how differently rulers treated the same shrine.
  - *Sources*: Satish Chandra, *Medieval India* I and II; J. L. Mehta, *Advanced Study in the History of Medieval India* I.
