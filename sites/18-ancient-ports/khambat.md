- **Location**: Khambhat, the Cambay of European sources, at the head of the Gulf of Khambhat where the Mahi meets the sea, Anand district, Gujarat - a funnel-shaped gulf with one of the world's highest tidal ranges.
- **Period/Level**: The dominant port of western India from about the 11th century to the 16th, under the Chaulukyas, the Gujarat Sultanate and then the Mughals. It was superseded by Surat in the 17th century as silting and the tidal bore closed the approach to larger ships.
- **Key Finds & Significance**: The Mughal empire's principal outlet to the western Indian Ocean, and a bead industry with four thousand years of continuity.
  - Marco Polo, Ibn Battuta, Duarte Barbosa and Tome Pires all describe it; Pires's remark that Cambay stretched out two arms, one towards Aden and the other towards Malacca, is the standard quotation for Gujarat's double orientation towards the Red Sea and Southeast Asia.
  - Exports were cotton textiles, chintz, indigo and carnelian beads; the return was gold, silver and horses from the Gulf, and spices from the east. Gujarati merchant capital financed much of it, which is why the port features in arguments about pre-colonial Indian commercial organisation.
  - Agate and carnelian from the Rajpipla hills are still cut and polished at Khambhat by heat-treating and drilling methods continuous with those used at Harappan Lothal, a short distance up the same gulf.
  - Its decline is environmental rather than political - a reminder that Indian port history is largely a history of silting rivers and shifting coastlines.
  - *Sources*: Upinder Singh, ch. 10; R. S. Sharma, *India's Ancient Past*; Satish Chandra, *Medieval India* II.
