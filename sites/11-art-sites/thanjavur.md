- **Location**: Thanjavur, Tamil Nadu, in the Kaveri delta on the Vadavaru branch - the Chola capital from Vijayalaya's capture of the town c. 850 CE, and the richest rice country in the peninsula.
- **Period/Level**: The Brihadishvara (Rajarajeshvara) temple was built by Rajaraja I between 1003 and 1010 CE, and the Chola murals in its inner ambulatory are of that date. Nayaka repainting over them belongs to the 16th century, after the fall of Vijayanagara; Maratha patronage runs from 1674. UNESCO World Heritage as one of the Great Living Chola Temples, 1987.
- **Key Finds & Significance**: The only substantial survival of Chola mural painting - hidden for nine centuries under a later layer, and the high point of Dravida temple building.
  - The 11th-century frescoes were found in 1931 by S. K. Govindasamy in the dark circumambulatory passage, painted beneath the Nayaka layer. They show Shiva as Tripurantaka, Nataraja at Chidambaram, the saint Sundarar with Cheraman Perumal, and figures taken to be Rajaraja I with his guru Karuvurar - among the earliest royal portraits in South Indian painting.
  - They are true fresco: pigment laid on still-damp lime plaster so that the colour bonds into the surface, which is why the Chola layer has outlasted the Nayaka work painted over it.
  - The temple, called the Dakshina Meru, carries a 66 m vimana of thirteen diminishing storeys over the sanctum - the tallest of its time - with the tower, not the gateway, as the dominant mass; the gopura only overtakes the vimana in later Pandya and Nayaka building. Its walls hold the largest corpus of Chola inscriptions anywhere, recording endowments, temple servants, musicians and the four hundred dancers transferred here, which is what makes Chola social and economic history writable at all.
  - Thanjavur painting proper is a later and separate tradition of the Maratha period, 18th-19th centuries - gesso relief, gold foil and inset glass or stones on a wooden panel, mostly Vaishnava subjects - and the reverse glass painting of the same workshops is equally well known.
  - *Sources*: Upinder Singh, ch. 10; Vidya Dehejia, *Art of the Imperial Cholas*; ASI, *South Indian Inscriptions* vol. II.
