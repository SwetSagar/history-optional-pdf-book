- **Location**: Kupgal, near Sanganakallu on the granite hills outside Ballari, Karnataka, in the Raichur doab between the Krishna and the Tungabhadra - the core of the South Indian Neolithic ashmound country.
- **Period/Level**: South Indian Neolithic with a Chalcolithic overlap, roughly 3000-1200 BCE. The site includes a hilltop settlement, ashmounds, thousands of petroglyphs, and the *rock gongs* for which it is best known. Re-surveyed in the 2000s by Nicole Boivin and colleagues after being lost to record for a century.
- **Key Finds & Significance**: A Neolithic ritual landscape - the clearest Indian evidence that prehistoric sites were used for ceremony as well as habitation.
  - The rock gongs are boulders bearing deep cup-shaped depressions which, when struck, ring with a loud metallic resonance. The wear on them shows sustained use, and their position beside the petroglyph concentrations argues that sound was part of whatever was performed here.
  - The petroglyphs, pecked rather than painted, are dominated by long-horned humped cattle, with human figures and hunting scenes. Cattle imagery, ashmounds and faunal remains together make the case that the southern Neolithic was organised around cattle herding, and that the ashmounds - great heaps of burnt cow dung - are the residue of periodic ritual burning of cattle pens rather than domestic rubbish.
  - Excavations at neighbouring Sanganakallu have produced ground stone axe workshops on an industrial scale, so the hills were a production centre as well as a ceremonial one.
  - The ceramics are burnished grey and buff wares, some painted in white, and copper and gold appear in later levels.
  - *Sources*: Upinder Singh, ch. 3; Thapar, *Early India*; Boivin et al., *Antiquity*.
