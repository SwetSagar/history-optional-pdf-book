- **Location**: Martand, near Mattan in Anantnag district, Kashmir, on a karewa plateau commanding the whole Jhelum valley - a deliberately chosen viewpoint rather than a settlement site.
- **Period/Level**: Built by Lalitaditya Muktapida of the Karkota dynasty, r. c. 724-760 CE, the high point of Kashmiri power, when Karkota armies campaigned into the plains and embassies went to Tang China. Ruined in the early 15th century under Sultan Sikandar Shah, called But-shikan.
- **Key Finds & Significance**: The definitive monument of the Kashmiri style - the one regional order in India that absorbs classical Mediterranean form.
  - The plan is a colonnaded quadrangle of eighty-four fluted columns enclosing a central shrine, a peristyle courtyard of a kind found nowhere else in Indian temple architecture. The columns are Greco-Roman in profile, and the trefoil arches within triangular pediments, with the steep double-pyramidal roof, come from Gandharan practice adapted to a snow climate.
  - It is the architectural expression of Kashmir's position on the routes between India, Iran and Central Asia. Lalitaditya's reign is also when the Karkotas were fielding armies across north India, so the temple is read as imperial statement as much as devotion.
  - Kalhana's *Rajatarangini*, written in 1149-50, records the foundation and the later destruction. That a twelfth-century Sanskrit chronicle can be checked against a standing building makes Kashmir unusually well documented for early medieval India.
  - Avantipur nearby, built by Avantivarman of the Utpala dynasty in the 9th century, continues the same style and is the natural comparison.
  - *Sources*: Upinder Singh, ch. 10; Thapar, *Early India*; Kalhana, *Rajatarangini* IV.
