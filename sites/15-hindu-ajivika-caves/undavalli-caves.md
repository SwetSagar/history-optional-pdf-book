- **Location**: Undavalli, Guntur district, Andhra Pradesh, cut into a sandstone hillside on the south bank of the Krishna, opposite Vijayawada - in the delta country that was the core of the Andhra kingdoms.
- **Period/Level**: Excavated from about the 4th-5th centuries CE and elaborated later, associated with the Vishnukundin dynasty, who ruled the Krishna-Godavari doab between the Satavahanas and the Eastern Chalukyas. The earliest cells are Buddhist; the surviving programme is Vaishnava.
- **Key Finds & Significance**: A four-storeyed rock-cut temple - the tallest such excavation in the south - and a clear record of a Buddhist site passing into Brahmanical use.
  - The facade rises in four tiers of pillared verandahs cut back into the hill, each storey set behind the one below. Nothing else in Indian rock-cut architecture is stacked this way, and it is the site's main architectural claim.
  - The second storey holds a colossal reclining Vishnu - Anantashayana, carved from a single block - together with panels of Vaishnava deities. It is among the largest monolithic images in southern India.
  - The lowest level retains plain cells and a pillared hall of the kind cut for Buddhist monks, with figures read as monastic. Andhra was the Buddhist heartland of Amaravati and Nagarjunakonda, and Undavalli shows what happened to that establishment as Vaishnavism became the dynastic religion under the Vishnukundins.
  - The Vishnukundins are otherwise known mainly from copper-plate grants, so Undavalli, with Mogalrajapuram nearby, supplies their architecture.
  - *Sources*: Upinder Singh, ch. 9 and 10; R. S. Sharma, *India's Ancient Past*; ASI.
