- **Location**: Thirunandikkara, Kanyakumari district, Tamil Nadu, near Thiruvattar in the Kodayar basin at the southern tip of the Western Ghats. The site lay in Travancore until the States Reorganisation of 1956, which is why its art belongs to the Kerala tradition though it is now in Tamil Nadu.
- **Period/Level**: A rock-cut cave temple of about the 8th-9th centuries CE, excavated under the Ay dynasty. It began as a Jaina shrine and was converted to Shaiva use as a Mahadeva temple; eleven later inscriptions of the imperial Cholas, recording endowments for the Sadaya festival, mark the period of Chola authority over Venad.
- **Key Finds & Significance**: The oldest surviving murals in the Kerala style - the starting point of a painting tradition that runs to Mattancherry and Padmanabhapuram.
  - The paintings, on the cave's inner walls, carry scenes from the *Ramayana* and *Mahabharata*. The method is visible in the surviving work: the figures were first drawn in outline and then filled with mineral colour, the technique later codified in the Kerala mural manuals.
  - They give the region an artistic sequence it otherwise lacks, standing between the Pallava-Pandya painting of Sittanavasal and the fully formed Kerala style of the 16th century.
  - The Jaina-to-Shaiva conversion is the site's other value. Jainism was strong in the far south in the early centuries CE and receded before the Shaiva bhakti of the Nayanmars; a single cave that changed hands documents that displacement physically.
  - The Chola inscriptions matter politically: they show imperial Chola reach into Venad, and are used to date the extension of Chola administration down the west coast.
  - *Sources*: Upinder Singh, ch. 10; ASI, *South Indian Inscriptions*; *Travancore Archaeological Series*.
