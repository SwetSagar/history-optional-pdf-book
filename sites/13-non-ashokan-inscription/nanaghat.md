- **Location**: The Naneghat pass through the Sahyadris, Junnar taluk, Pune district, Maharashtra - the route carrying goods up from the Konkan ports of Kalyan and Sopara to Junnar and Pratishthana, at the head of the Kukadi valley. The inscription is in a rock-cut cave beside the pass.
- **Period/Level**: A Brahmi inscription in Prakrit of about the 1st century BCE, issued by the Satavahana queen Naganika, widow of Satakarni I, during the minority of her son - the formative phase of Satavahana power in the western Deccan.
- **Key Finds & Significance**: The founding document of Satavahana history, and the earliest attestation of the numerals from which modern digits descend.
  - It lists the Vedic sacrifices performed by the royal house - the rajasuya, two ashvamedhas, the agnyadheya and others - with the dakshina paid for each in thousands of cows, horses, elephants, villages and karshapanas: the fullest record of shrauta sacrifice by any historical Indian dynasty, and the best surviving index of what royal ritual cost.
  - That the same dynasty endowed the Buddhist caves at Nashik, Karle and Kanheri while performing ashvamedhas is the standard demonstration that royal patronage was not confined to one religion - the point to make on Satavahana religious policy.
  - Naganika issues the record in her own name as regent, among the earliest instances of a queen exercising political authority in Indian epigraphy. Portrait sculptures of the royal family stood in the cave; the figures are lost, but their labels survive, naming Simuka, Satakarni and others.
  - Its numerals are the earliest well-attested Brahmi forms, ancestral to the Indian and then the Arabic digits. A cistern at the pass collected tolls, tying inscription, trade route and revenue together in one place.
  - *Sources*: Upinder Singh, ch. 8; D. C. Sircar, *Select Inscriptions*; Thapar, *Early India*.
