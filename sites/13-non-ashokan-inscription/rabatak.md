- **Location**: Rabatak, near Surkh Kotal in Baghlan province, northern Afghanistan - ancient Bactria, north of the Hindu Kush in the Kunduz drainage of the Oxus, the Kushana heartland.
- **Period/Level**: A stone inscription of Kanishka I from the first year of his reign, c. 127 CE, in the Bactrian language written in the Greek script - the opening of the Kushana empire's greatest phase. Discovered in 1993.
- **Key Finds & Significance**: The find that settled the Kushana genealogy after a century of scholarly argument - the most consequential Indian epigraphic discovery of recent decades.
  - It states the succession outright: Kujula Kadphises his great-grandfather, Vima Taktu his grandfather, Vima Kadphises his father, and Kanishka himself. Vima Taktu was previously unknown, and his insertion resolved the long dispute over the order and number of the early Kushana kings.
  - Kanishka says he issued the edict in Greek and then put it into Aryan - that is, Bactrian. This is an explicit record of a deliberate change of administrative language, from the Greek inherited from the Bactrian Greeks to the local Iranian vernacular, and the clearest statement of language policy in ancient Indian epigraphy.
  - It records the founding of a sanctuary to the goddess Nana with a list of Iranian deities, and images of the king and his ancestors installed in it - a dynastic cult of the same kind as the devakula at Mat near Mathura.
  - It names cities brought under submission, among them Saketa, Kaushambi, Pataliputra and Champa, the main epigraphic evidence for how far Kushana authority reached into the Ganga plain.
  - *Sources*: Upinder Singh, ch. 8; Sims-Williams and Cribb, "A New Bactrian Inscription of Kanishka the Great"; Thapar, *Early India*.
