- **Location**: The Iron Pillar, now in the courtyard of the Quwwat-ul-Islam mosque in the Qutb complex at Mehrauli, south Delhi, on the ridge west of the Yamuna. It was brought here; the inscription says it was raised on a hill called Vishnupada.
- **Period/Level**: A Sanskrit inscription in Gupta Brahmi of the 4th-5th century CE naming a king Chandra, generally identified with Chandragupta II, r. c. 375-415 CE - the height of Gupta power. The shaft stands about 7.2 m above ground and weighs over six tonnes.
- **Key Finds & Significance**: The most famous metallurgical object of ancient India, and a political record of Gupta expansion into Bengal and the north-west.
  - It has not rusted through in some sixteen centuries. The accepted explanation is a thin protective film of crystalline iron hydrogen phosphate, formed because the iron was made by direct reduction, is high in phosphorus and free of sulphur, and because the pillar was forge-welded from lumps of wrought iron rather than cast - large-scale forging Europe did not match for another millennium.
  - The inscription records that King Chandra broke a confederacy of enemies in Vanga (Bengal), crossed the seven mouths of the Indus and conquered the Vahlikas (Bactrians). These campaigns are otherwise poorly documented, so the pillar is a primary source for the reach of Chandragupta II.
  - It was set up as a *dhvaja*, a standard of Vishnu, once surmounted by a Garuda image now lost - the Vaishnava affiliation of the Guptas made material.
  - Its removal to the Qutb complex makes it, with Firoz Shah Tughlaq's Ashokan pillars, a second case of a Sultanate ruler collecting an ancient monument as a trophy of legitimacy.
  - *Sources*: Upinder Singh, ch. 9; J. F. Fleet, *Corpus Inscriptionum Indicarum* III; R. Balasubramaniam, *Delhi Iron Pillar*.
