- **Location**: Budihal, Kalaburagi (Gulbarga) district, northern Karnataka, on the dry Deccan plateau in the upper Bhima basin - within the ashmound belt.
- **Period/Level**: A South Indian Neolithic settlement of roughly the 2nd millennium BCE, excavated by K. Paddayya of Deccan College across four separate localities within one site.
- **Key Finds & Significance**: The excavation that reinterpreted ashmounds as parts of living settlements, and the best evidence in Indian prehistory for communal food sharing.
  - Paddayya exposed a large area rather than a trench, and recovered a functioning settlement plan: an ashmound at the centre formed by cattle penning and dung burning, separate dung disposal areas, living floors, a chert blade workshop on the sandstone boulders nearby, and burials within the habitation.
  - The most striking feature is a communal butchering floor - a paved area scattered with the bones of cattle, sheep and goat, with blades and choppers, where animals were dismembered and the meat apparently distributed. Organised meat processing on this scale implies collective activity and sharing beyond the household, which is very hard to demonstrate archaeologically.
  - The work shifted the ashmound debate decisively. Allchin had shown what ashmounds were made of; Paddayya showed where they sat in the life of a community - at the centre of settlements, not apart from them.
  - Red and grey pottery, ground stone axes, chert blades and bone tools complete the assemblage.
  - *Sources*: Upinder Singh, ch. 3; R. S. Sharma, *India's Ancient Past*; K. Paddayya, *Man and Environment*.
