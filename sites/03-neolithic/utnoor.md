- **Location**: Utnur, Adilabad district, Telangana, on the Deccan plateau in the upper Godavari basin - in the northern part of the ashmound belt that runs across the southern Deccan.
- **Period/Level**: A South Indian Neolithic ashmound excavated by F. R. Allchin in 1957, with radiocarbon dates in the 3rd millennium BCE. The excavation is the decisive one for understanding what ashmounds are.
- **Key Finds & Significance**: The dig that settled a century of speculation about the Deccan ashmounds.
  - Ashmounds - great heaps of grey vitrified material, some many metres high - had been explained as volcanic, as meteoritic, as iron-smelting slag and as the residue of brick kilns. Allchin's excavation at Utnur found post-holes marking a substantial wooden stockade enclosing an area filled with accumulated cattle dung, burnt and reburnt over long periods.
  - Ashmounds are therefore cattle pens whose dung was periodically fired, probably as a seasonal ritual and practically to control parasites and clear the ground. The interpretation is now standard, and Utnur is the evidence for it.
  - The finding transformed the picture of the southern Neolithic from one of settled cereal farmers into one of cattle-keeping communities for whom herds were both wealth and religious focus - a reading supported by the cattle imagery in the rock art at Kupgal and Piklihal.
  - The site also produced ground stone axes, blade tools and handmade coarse pottery.
  - Later work by Paddayya at Budihal refined this by showing ashmounds as components of settlements rather than isolated monuments.
  - *Sources*: Upinder Singh, ch. 3; Thapar, *Early India*; F. R. Allchin, *Neolithic Cattle-Keepers of South India*.
