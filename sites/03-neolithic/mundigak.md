- **Location**: Mundigak, about 55 km north-west of Kandahar in southern Afghanistan, on the Kushk-i-Nakhud river - an oasis mound on the routes linking Iran, Central Asia and the Indus.
- **Period/Level**: Seven periods excavated by Jean-Marie Casal, running from about 4000 BCE to the early 2nd millennium, with later reoccupation. The site's peak is Period IV, in the middle of the 3rd millennium.
- **Key Finds & Significance**: A major regional centre outside the Indus system, and evidence that monumental building was not a Harappan monopoly.
  - The lowest levels have no structures at all, only hearths and pits, indicating a semi-nomadic camp that later became permanent - one of the clearer archaeological records of sedentarisation.
  - Period IV has a large building with a facade of engaged pilasters, usually called the palace, and a massive walled structure read as a temple, on a scale unmatched in the region. Monumental architecture here, contemporary with the Harappan cities, shows comparable social complexity developing along the Helmand and Kandahar routes.
  - Finds include alabaster vessels, terracotta humped bulls, copper needles and blades, and a carved stone head sometimes compared with the Mohenjo-daro priest-king.
  - Wheat and ber among the plant remains, with bones of domesticated animals, complete the subsistence picture.
  - Mundigak is normally studied with Shahr-i-Sokhta in Iran and the Helmand civilisation as part of the world that supplied the Indus with lapis, tin and turquoise.
  - *Sources*: Upinder Singh, ch. 3 and 4; R. S. Sharma, *India's Ancient Past*; Casal, *Fouilles de Mundigak*.
